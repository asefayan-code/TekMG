Capacitor Replacement: Why We Don’t Replace Every Capacitor on Every Unit

Tek Media Group has replaced capacitors in vintage amplifiers, receivers, tape decks and Bang & Olufsen equipment for 30 years. We replace the capacitors that are failing or under stress, test every new part before it goes in, and protect the aging circuit board. Vintage tube equipment gets all electrolytic and paper capacitors replaced for safety.

What targeted replacement gives you

  • Restored factory performance. Only the capacitors that are out of specification are replaced.
  • Hum and noise removed. Worn power supply capacitors are the main cause.
  • Failures prevented. The capacitors that are actually failing are replaced. The rest are fine.
  • Long-term reliability. Modern 105°C low-ESR capacitors in the critical positions outlast the originals.
  • The vintage character stays. The sound is cleaner and tighter only where it matters.
  • You pay for what the unit needs.

What a full recap adds

  • No audible improvement from replacing healthy capacitors on rails that are not stressed.
  • No reliability gain. Capacitors that have survived 30+ years are burned in. New parts restart the early-failure period.
  • Several times the labor and parts for the same result.
  • More risk to the circuit board. Every extra joint means more heat, more trace stress and more lifted pads.
  • A changed sound. Modern low-ESR capacitors can make vintage equipment sound harsh or clinical.
  • Film, ceramic and mica capacitors replaced for no reason. These types do not degrade.

Why we don’t replace every capacitor on every board

  • One failed capacitor does not condemn the rest. A capacitor usually fails from local heat or a bad production batch.
  • Heat damages old boards. Each pad heated past 300°C can lift a trace, damage a via or delaminate the board. Twenty capacitors mean forty solder joints.
  • Survivors are reliable. Parts that have run for years are past the early-failure period. New parts are not.
  • Some circuits are sensitive. In switching power supplies, capacitors affect timing and damping. We match ESR and ripple ratings where it matters.
  • New capacitors can be stale. A capacitor that sat on a shelf for years may need reforming before use.
  • A working original board is worth more than a damaged one that was recapped. Some boards are too fragile to touch, and we say so.

Risks with aging circuit boards

  • Lifted and broken traces. Old solder does not flow and heat lingers, most of all on phenolic boards from the 1960s and 1970s.
  • Cracked plated-through holes. On 1970s and 1980s boards the thin copper inside the holes cracks with heat and causes intermittent faults.
  • Loose solder pads that float and spin when a new part is fitted.
  • Heat damage to nearby parts such as plastic transistors and diodes.
  • Brittle wires and connectors. 40-year-old insulation and locking tabs snap.
  • Cracked joints from flexing the board.
  • Static damage to logic chips in late 1970s and 1980s equipment.
  • Old glue under capacitors that has turned conductive and corrosive. It all has to come off.
  • Polarity markings printed wrong on some boards. We check against the schematic.

Parts and testing

  • Parts. Low ESR, 105°C rated capacitors from Nichicon, Panasonic, Elna or Vishay. Coupling capacitors are often film types from WIMA or Panasonic.
  • Testing before installation. Every capacitor is checked with an ESR meter and a capacitance tester. We reject a part that is more than 10% off its capacitance or more than 20% above its typical datasheet ESR.
  • Inspection. Every part is checked for bent leads, cracked bases and dented cans.
  • Ceramic disc capacitors are left alone unless cracked or shorted.
  • First power-up is always through a dim-bulb tester.
  • Sound. Two identical receivers can sound different after the same work. It depends on the circuit, the capacitor series and the board revision. We discuss this with you first.
